Understanding Alzheimer’s

Most of what people believe about Alzheimer’s disease is seen from the outside. This room is for the inside. Read slowly. Nothing here asks for pity. It asks for recognition.

−0200 M · WHAT IT IS

Alzheimer’s disease is the most common cause of Dementia (Mayo Clinic, 2026). There are early, middle, and late-stage symptoms of the disease as it gets progressively worse. General symptoms for Alzheimer’s are:

  • MemoryThe question is new every time it is asked.
  • Thinking and reasoningThe thought is there. The road to it is not.
  • Making judgments and decisionsTwo coats on the hook, and no way left to choose between them.
  • Planning and performing familiar tasksThe wish is whole. The steps arrive out of order.
  • Changes in personality and behaviorThe temper is not new. The patience it used to have is gone.
−0800 M · HOW MANY

There’s no cure for Alzheimer’s disease at present, and it affects a considerable portion of the world’s population. It is shown that “an estimated 40 million people, mostly older than 60 years, have dementia worldwide, and this figure is projected to double every 20 years, until at least 2050.” (Scheltens et al., 2016, p. 506). It’s a very relevant and serious issue all around the world.

−1600 M · WHAT IS LOST

Living with Alzheimer’s disease is a difficult experience to be sure. Memory and cognition are chief components of our invaluable consciousness. One patient cites:

“I don’t know where my head is, it’s driving me mad. I can’t remember anything.”
AGGARWAL ET AL., 2003, P. 193

People affected by the disease not only lose cognitive abilities, but their autonomy as well, sadly.

−2400 M · INDEPENDENCE

The importance of independence and a functioning autonomy in a person’s life can be argued to be overlooked, as it’s thought a given any adult should have. Having to be accompanied to most places, and generally not being able to care for yourself properly is a shared experience in Alzheimer’s in its progressive stages. As Aggarwal argues: “as the disease progressed, independence was gradually lost.” (Aggarwal et al., 2003, p. 192). So, it’s not just memory loss or decline that comes with the experience of Alzheimer’s.

−3000 M · COMMUNICATION

And to give one final example for the scope of the experience, communication also deteriorates. Being able to communicate is another core aspect of everyday life, as basic as it is, so much depends on it. “People with dementia could not always communicate to others how they felt.” (Aggarwal et al., 2003, p. 193). Family members and loved ones not being able to relate or understand the patient because of their difficulties communicating is a massive, and an understandably crushing problem for them. The patients suffer both on an emotional plane because of this disconnect, and in a practical manner, because at times they are unable to communicate their immediate needs as well.

−3400 M · MISUNDERSTOOD, NOT PITIED

None of this asks you to feel sorry. Pity looks at a person from above. Understanding stands next to them in the water. A person with Alzheimer’s is not fading into someone else. They are navigating a sea the rest of us have never had to swim, with less light than we are given, and mostly without complaint.
